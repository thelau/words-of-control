# Round 6 — journalist (preview review, as it would run)

*Material seen: docs/captures/review9 sheets (42, baby, fuck, grief, joy, love, rage, spoon, table, war; kiss in progress), audio spectrograms (joy, war, table and others), references/craft contact sheets.*

---

## Say "war" and it performs for you. Say "love" and it barely looks up.

**Laurent's *Words of Control* hands a machine one word and a single job: to judge it. It is at its best, and most unsettling, when the word is ugly.**

In an empty room you sit at a typewriter a long way from a large screen. You type a word and press Enter. For a second or two the screen turns into a Ryoji Ikeda-style readout: barcodes, columns of four-decimal numbers, bar charts on bone-white paper. These are the forty-nine answers an AI model gave about your word, flashed faster than you can read them. A single hairline holds the centre. Then comes the "verdict": nine to fourteen seconds of point clouds seen through a macro lens, a slab of cracked relief, sand settling into Chladni figures. After that the screen goes black, the reverb tails off and you are back in the room.

The frames are handsome. The Chladni shots, pale clay ridges under a single hard light, are the best images in the piece. A monolith of dried-mud relief rotating in the dark lands somewhere between Kubrick and a geology museum. The sound is well built too: sub-bass hits you feel in your chest, glassy pings around 1 kHz, a sheet of hiss that stops dead at every cut.

What stays with you, though, is how unequal the attention is. *War*, *rage* and *fuck* each get five camera setups: particle strands, drifting bokeh, relief, sand, and a red-flecked city of points under a wall of low end. *Joy* and *baby* get two. *Love* gets a few dim pink and teal smudges of bokeh and then black. I can't tell whether this is a thesis or an accident, but the piece currently argues that a judging machine finds hate more interesting than tenderness. If that is the point, I would like it to be stated. If it isn't, it needs fixing.

Neutral words have a sameness problem. *Spoon*, *table* and *42* all open on the same glowing vertical blade crossing a field of dust. The spectrograms show a neutral word and a joyful one sharing the same four pumping sub-bass pulses. On the evidence of these captures, forty-nine questions come out as three moods. Many of the negative frames are sparse bokeh on near-black, which reads as a gap in the edit, not as deliberate restraint. The artist's own reference reels, dense and luminous and saturated, are richer than what the room delivers.

The work keeps its central promise: nothing you see or hear is generated. The model's only contribution is a set of numbers, and every image and sound was authored. That is a real ethical position and a rare one now. The trouble is that the judgement itself is hard to see. The appraisal passes too quickly to read, and the verdict is too broad to tell apart. You come away knowing the machine had an opinion, but not what it was.

A serious, well-made work that is one decisive edit away from being a sharp one.

## Notes to the artist: what would make this a rave (ranked)

1. **Fix the attention imbalance, or own it.** Negative words get five shots and the richest material; positive words get two thin ones, and *love* is the weakest verdict in the set. Either give warmth the same range of forms and the same length, or make the imbalance the stated subject. Right now it reads as a bug.
2. **Make one appraisal answer legible.** Let a single number, question or bar hold for about 600 ms, long enough to read, so the visitor sees that this word was judged on this point. Without it, the 49 answers are texture and the concept is invisible.
3. **Stop neutral words from all looking the same.** Spoon, table and 42 open on the same vertical blade; joy and table share the same sub-bass pulse pattern. The 49 values have to steer form, framing and rhythm, not just a three-way mood switch.
4. **Cut the dead bokeh frames.** Sparse discs on near-black make up about a quarter of the negative verdicts. Your references are dense and luminous; bring the particle clouds up to that density or cut those shots.
5. **Give the verdict an arc, not a slideshow.** The shots are a series of cuts with the same weight. Build one moment of peak intensity per word, a collapse or an ignition, and keep the constant hiss sheet from flattening the whole thing.
6. **Keep the typed word in view longer.** It shows small and briefly above a hex string, and for some words it is replaced by a glyph. Before the machine takes the word apart, let the visitor see it at scale for a moment.
